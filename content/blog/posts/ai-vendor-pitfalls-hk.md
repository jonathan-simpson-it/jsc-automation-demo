**Most small firms that buy AI are sold a tool, not a result, and the difference shows up in the first renewal.** The seven pitfalls below cover the procurement mistakes we see repeatedly at boutique asset managers, IAMs, and family offices in Hong Kong: buying before defining, paying for features nobody uses, and signing contracts that hand client data to vendors without the protections that matter.

The good news is that each pitfall has a counterpart checklist item. This guide is written for the buyer side of the table: what to ask, what to demand in the contract, and how to price a tool against the work it replaces rather than the vendor's list.

## Pitfall 1: Buying before mapping the process

The vendor demo is the most expensive hour in procurement. A demo shows the tool at its best; it does not show your process, your data, or your exceptions. Firms that skip the process map buy tools that automate the parts that were already fine.

**The fix:** document the workflow before the demo: inputs, steps, owners, time per step, error points. Then evaluate the tool against the map, and ask the vendor to run *your* process, not theirs.

## Pitfall 2: Confusing features with outcomes

Vendors sell feature lists; firms need outcomes. "AI-powered," "machine learning," and "copilot" appear on everything, and most features in a modern SaaS product are never opened after onboarding.

**The fix:** list the three to five outcomes that matter: cycle time, error rate, review hours, coverage. Score every tool against the outcomes. A tool that does one outcome brilliantly beats a platform that does ten poorly.

## Pitfall 3: Ignoring the data question

The single most important question for a regulated firm: **what happens to client data?** Many vendors cannot answer cleanly: data is processed offshore, stored for "product improvement," or shared with subprocessors the firm has never heard of. For HK firms this collides directly with PCPD obligations and vendor due diligence expectations.

**The fix:** demand written answers to: where is data processed and stored, does it train the model, what is the retention period, who are the subprocessors, how is deletion verified, and what happens on termination. If the answers are vague, walk.

## Pitfall 4: Pricing against the wrong benchmark

Vendor pricing feels reasonable next to another vendor's pricing. It only means something next to the cost of the work being replaced. A tool priced below a headcount can still be a bad buy if it replaces nothing.

**The fix:** price per workflow or per cycle against the current cost of that work: time cost, error cost, and management review. The reference economics: a deployed pipeline runs roughly 65% below the fully loaded cost of a junior analyst.

## Pitfall 5: Signing the template contract

Vendor templates are written for the vendor. The gaps that hurt small firms: no data processing terms, no security commitments, no audit rights, liability capped at a month of fees, and termination terms that make leaving expensive.

**The fix:** insist on the core protections: scope of processing, security and deletion commitments, audit rights, service levels with remedies, reasonable liability, and clean termination with data return. These are standard in enterprise procurement; small firms are entitled to the same protections, and serious vendors will negotiate.

## Pitfall 6: Skipping the pilot

The demo is not a pilot. Firms that sign after a demo and discover problems in month two have already paid for month one and the onboarding effort.

**The fix:** run a time-boxed pilot on your data, with your team, with pre-agreed pass thresholds, and make the contract contingent on it. A vendor that resists a pilot is signalling the tool will not survive one.

## Pitfall 7: No one owns the vendor

"Everyone is responsible" means no one is. Post-purchase, the tool either has a named owner (someone accountable for usage, renewal, and results), or it drifts into a shelf-ware subscription that renews silently.

**The fix:** name an owner before signing: responsible for rollout, measurement against the pre-agreed metrics, the renewal decision, and termination if the tool does not deliver.

## The procurement checklist

- [ ] Process mapped and baseline measured before any demo
- [ ] Three to five outcomes defined, scored per candidate
- [ ] Written data answers: location, training, retention, subprocessors, deletion
- [ ] Pricing benchmarked against the cost of the work replaced
- [ ] Contract reviewed for the core protections listed above
- [ ] Pilot agreed with pre-defined pass thresholds
- [ ] Named owner with renewal authority and termination duty

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the biggest mistake small firms make buying AI?**
Buying a tool before defining the process. Firms that map the workflow and baseline the current cost first consistently get better outcomes than firms that start from demos and feature lists.

**What should you ask an AI vendor about data?**
Where data is processed and stored, whether it trains the model, what happens on termination, subprocessors, and how deletion is verified. If the vendor cannot answer in writing, that is the answer.

**How do you know if an AI price is fair?**
Price against the cost of the work it replaces (per-cycle or per-headcount), not against other list prices. Reference economics are roughly 65% below a fully loaded junior analyst.

**What contract terms matter most for AI?**
Scope of processing, security and deletion commitments, audit rights, service levels, liability allocation, termination and data return, and change control.

## Buy like you are being audited on the purchase
